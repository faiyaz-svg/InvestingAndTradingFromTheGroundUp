## Part 1: How markets work

### [What a stock is and why prices move](../01-how-markets-work/01-what-a-stock-is.md)

- Thinking the price is "fair" because it is the price. It is the last agreed trade, which can be wrong within minutes.
- Assuming good news means the price rises. The price already holds what the market expected, so only the surprise moves it.
- Reading market cap as money the company has. It is what shareholders' stakes are worth at the current price.

### [Exchanges and where your order goes](../01-how-markets-work/02-exchanges-and-where-orders-go.md)

- Treating an exchange's price as the market price. The best price can be on any venue, which is why the NBBO exists.
- Assuming a "free" commission means a free trade. The cost can sit in the spread and the fill price, so compare the fills as well as the commissions.
- Quoting the market's venue count as a fixed number. New exchanges open and old ones merge, so look it up when you need it.

### [Market hours and the opening and closing auctions](../01-how-markets-work/03-market-hours-and-auctions.md)

- Placing a market order in extended hours and being filled far from the last price. Use a limit order (lesson 5) and check your broker's rules.
- Reading the opening print as the day's "real" price. The first trade of a stock comes from an auction with its own imbalance.
- Forgetting an early close. Check the exchange calendar before a holiday week.

### [Bid, ask and the order book](../01-how-markets-work/04-bid-ask-and-the-order-book.md)

- Trading off the "last price" and expecting it. The last trade can be on either side of the spread.
- Looking only at the spread. A one-cent spread with 100 shares at each price is cheap for 100 shares and expensive for 5,000.
- Treating displayed size as certain. Orders can be cancelled the moment before you trade.

### [Order types](../01-how-markets-work/05-order-types.md)

- Using a market order on a thin stock or in extended hours. Use a marketable limit so you decide the worst price.
- Setting a stop at a round number a few cents below the market. Normal noise can trigger it, and the stop places no limit on the price.
- Forgetting that a day order dies at the close. A limit that did not fill is gone tomorrow.

### [Who is on the other side of your trade](../01-how-markets-work/06-who-is-on-the-other-side.md)

- Assuming your order is matched with another individual. It is usually matched with a firm that is managing inventory.
- Treating a tight spread as no cost. A tight spread is small on each share, and it is paid on both the entry and the exit.
- Using limit orders and assuming they always avoid the spread. A limit at the bid waits in line and may fill only on a falling price.

### [Settlement, cash accounts and margin](../01-how-markets-work/07-settlement-cash-and-margin.md)

- Sizing by buying power. Margin allows a larger position than your risk plan does, and a bigger position loses faster.
- Forgetting interest. It accrues each day you hold the loan, so holding a leveraged position for months costs real money.
- Assuming you get a warning before a forced sale. A broker may sell without calling first when equity is below the requirement.

### [Short selling](../01-how-markets-work/08-short-selling.md)

- Sizing a short like a long. A 50% rise costs 50% of the proceeds, and a short has no cap, so size it by the stop you can honour.
- Ignoring the borrow fee. On a hard-to-borrow stock the fee can exceed the move you expect.
- Shorting into a crowded trade. A high days to cover means a rise can force the exit of many shorts at once.

### [Dividends, splits and corporate actions](../01-how-markets-work/09-dividends-splits-and-corporate-actions.md)

- Buying on the ex-date to "catch" the dividend. You needed to own the stock the business day before.
- Reading a split as a crash or a gain. The adjusted chart shows what happened to value.
- Treating a high dividend yield as a gift. A yield rises when the price falls, so it can signal trouble.

### [Indexes, sectors and market cap](../01-how-markets-work/10-indexes-sectors-and-market-cap.md)

- Reading the Dow as "the market". It has 30 stocks and weights by price, so a US$400 stock counts more than a US$40 stock.
- Comparing an index's price level to your portfolio's return. An index level may exclude dividends. Check whether you are looking at a price index or a total return index.
- Treating sector labels as fixed. They change, and an ETF's sector weights can shift without any trade.

### [Reading a quote page](../01-how-markets-work/11-reading-a-quote-page.md)

- Reading a delayed quote as live. Free pages can run 15 minutes behind, so look for a "delayed" label.
- Ignoring the volume. A price move on very low volume says little.


## Part 2: Accounts, currency and taxes

### [Account types for trading](../02-accounts-currency-and-taxes/01-account-types-for-trading.md)

- Trading very often in a TFSA without knowing that the CRA can treat frequent trading as business income.
- Counting on a loss inside a TFSA, RRSP or FHSA to offset gains elsewhere. It does not.
- Withdrawing from a TFSA and putting the money back in the same year. The room returns only on January 1.

### [Wealthsimple and IBKR compared](../02-accounts-currency-and-taxes/02-wealthsimple-and-ibkr.md)

- Comparing commissions only. The conversion fee on a US trade is often larger than any commission.
- Taking a fee from a review or blog. Brokers change fees; read the broker's own page and note the date.
- Treating CIPF as insurance against losing money in the market. It is not.

### [USD or CAD, and currency conversion fees](../02-accounts-currency-and-taxes/03-usd-vs-cad-and-fx-fees.md)

- Judging cost by the headline fee. A 1.5% fee charged on both the buy and the sell is about 3% of the position.
- Converting back to CAD after each trade. In a USD account the fee returns every time you do, so keep the US dollars while you trade US stocks.
- Treating the Bank of Canada rate as what you could have got. It is an average of mid quotes, not a price.

### [Norbert's gambit and CDRs](../02-accounts-currency-and-taxes/04-norberts-gambit-and-cdrs.md)

- Starting the gambit with a small amount. Fixed fees make it more expensive than the broker's fee below a certain size, which you can compute.
- Using market orders on DLR or DLR.U. A limit order caps the spread you pay.
- Forgetting that the shares cannot be sold while the journal is pending, so the DLR.U price can move before you sell.

### [Currency risk on US stocks](../02-accounts-currency-and-taxes/05-currency-risk-on-us-stocks.md)

- Adding the stock return and the currency return. They multiply, so use the formula.
- Thinking a USD account means no currency risk. It changes the day you convert, not the exposure.
- Ignoring the currency on a long hold. Over years it can be as large as the stock's move.

### [US withholding tax and the W-8BEN](../02-accounts-currency-and-taxes/06-us-withholding-tax-and-w-8ben.md)

- Skipping the W-8BEN or letting it expire, which can leave 30% withheld instead of 15%.
- Forgetting that US dividends in a TFSA or FHSA carry a cost that never comes back.
- Reporting only the net dividend in a cash account. You report the gross amount and claim the credit.

### [Capital gains or business income](../02-accounts-currency-and-taxes/07-capital-gains-vs-business-income.md)

- Assuming that holding for more than a year makes a gain a capital gain. Holding period is one factor, not a rule.
- Assuming a TFSA ends the question. The CRA can tax business income in a TFSA.
- Picking the treatment each year by whichever costs less. The CRA looks at the pattern of your activity, and a switch needs a reason.

### [Cost base in CAD and keeping records](../02-accounts-currency-and-taxes/08-cost-base-in-cad-and-records.md)

- Computing the gain in US dollars and converting once. The CRA wants each leg at its own date's rate.
- Resetting the average cost after a sale. A sale does not change the average of what remains.
- Using a different rate source from year to year.

### [Tax losses and the superficial loss rule](../02-accounts-currency-and-taxes/09-tax-losses-and-superficial-loss.md)

- Selling at a loss in a cash account and buying the same stock in your TFSA or RRSP. The loss is generally treated as denied, with no sure way to recover it.
- Forgetting the 30 days before the sale. A purchase on March 5 and a sale on March 10 can be superficial.
- Leaving a DRIP on in an account that holds the stock you sold.

### [Margin rules in Canada](../02-accounts-currency-and-taxes/10-margin-rules-in-canada.md)

- Sizing the loan by what the broker allows. The call comes much sooner than a full fall in the position.
- Leaving out the exchange rate when the position is a US stock.
- Assuming a margin call gives you time to deposit funds.


## Part 3: Risk and position sizing

### [Risk first: the math of losses](../03-risk-and-position-sizing/01-risk-first.md)

- Adding losses instead of compounding them. Four 10% losses are 34.39%, and the gain needed is 52.4%, not 40%.
- Judging a loss by its size alone. A 20% loss needs a 25% gain, and a 50% loss needs a 100% gain: twice the loss, four times the climb.
- Thinking a high-risk trade can be "made up" later. The deeper the hole, the more the account has to earn on a smaller base.

### [R-multiples](../03-risk-and-position-sizing/02-r-multiples.md)

- Calling a trade "+2R" when the stop was moved after entry. R is fixed at entry from the first stop. A later, wider stop does not change it.
- Measuring risk from the price you wish you had paid. Use the price you actually got.
- Treating R as a fixed dollar amount across the account's life. R is whatever you risked on that trade (lesson 4 sets it as a percent of the account).

### [Win rate, payoff and expectancy](../03-risk-and-position-sizing/03-win-rate-payoff-and-expectancy.md)

- Reading a win rate without the payoff ratio. A 70% win rate with small winners can lose money.
- Trusting an expectancy from 15 or 20 trades. The error bar is wider than the edge.
- Mixing R and dollars. Compute expectancy in R, then convert with the dollar value of one R.

### [Position sizing with fixed fractional risk](../03-risk-and-position-sizing/04-fixed-fractional-sizing.md)

- Sizing by what you can afford. Cost tells you nothing about the loss at the stop.
- Rounding to the nearest share instead of down. Over many trades the extra risk adds up.
- Choosing the stop after choosing the size. The stop comes from the chart (lesson 5), and the size follows from it. If the stop is too wide for the size you want, the answer is a smaller position or no trade.

### [Where to put the stop](../03-risk-and-position-sizing/05-where-to-put-the-stop.md)

- Putting the stop exactly on the low. A price that touches the level and turns can stop you out. Leave a buffer.
- Widening the stop after entry to avoid being stopped. That changes the risk you agreed to (lesson 2 keeps R fixed at entry). If the idea is still right, you should have placed the stop there to begin with.
- Picking a stop from a round loss, such as "I will lose 2%". A percent of price has no link to the chart.

### [Slippage, gaps and costs](../03-risk-and-position-sizing/06-slippage-gaps-and-costs.md)

- Treating -1R as the worst case. A stop can fill worse, and a gap can fill much worse.
- Ignoring costs on tight-stop, short-term trades. A cost of US$0.04 on a US$0.40 stop is 0.1R on every trade.
- Using the stop price in the journal when the fill was lower. Record the real fill, or the R-multiples will look better than they were.

### [Portfolio heat and correlation](../03-risk-and-position-sizing/07-portfolio-heat-and-correlation.md)

- Checking each trade's risk and never the total. Six 1% trades are 6% at risk.
- Counting positions in the same sector as separate bets. Group them and cap the group.
- Forgetting that a stop moved to break-even still leaves open profit at risk. Count from the current price.

### [Drawdowns and risk of ruin](../03-risk-and-position-sizing/08-drawdowns-and-risk-of-ruin.md)

- Planning for the average drawdown. The median maximum drawdown is the middle case, and half of the paths are worse.
- Quoting a risk of ruin without its assumptions. The formula needs a known win rate, fixed bet size and no change in the edge.
- Raising the risk after a winning run. The edge did not change, but the size of the swings did.

### [The Kelly criterion and fractional Kelly](../03-risk-and-position-sizing/09-kelly-and-fractional-kelly.md)

- Treating the Kelly fraction as the right size. It is the peak of a curve built on exact inputs you do not have.
- Using a win rate measured on a small sample. An error of a few points can turn an edge into a loss.
- Betting more than Kelly. Growth falls past the peak and drawdowns get deeper.


## Part 4: Psychology, the journal and paper trading

### [Biases that cost traders money](../04-psychology-journal-and-paper-trading/01-biases-that-cost-traders.md)

- Treating "I know about this bias" as protection. Knowing the name does not change the feeling. Only a rule you set before the trade does.
- Moving the stop a little while the trade is open. The stop was set when you were calm, so it is the better number.
- Calling a held loser a long-term investment. If you would not buy it today as an investment, you are holding it because of the sunk cost.

### [Tilt, revenge trading and FOMO](../04-psychology-journal-and-paper-trading/02-tilt-revenge-and-fomo.md)

- Setting a daily loss limit and then ignoring it once. The day you ignore it is the day it was written for.
- Taking the next trade at the same size to "prove" the loss was normal. A short break works better than a quick trade.
- Treating a missed trade as a loss. You did not lose money, you did not take a trade.

### [Rules and checklists](../04-psychology-journal-and-paper-trading/03-rules-and-checklists.md)

- Writing rules that need judgment, such as "only trade strong stocks". Replace it with a test you can run, such as "the stock is above its 50-day average" (defined in Part 5, [Moving averages](../05-reading-charts/05-moving-averages.md)).
- Changing a rule in the middle of a drawdown. Change rules only at the weekly review, after a loss streak is over.
- Making the checklist so long that you skip it. Eight items are enough. Add one only when a mistake repeats.

### [The trade journal](../04-psychology-journal-and-paper-trading/04-the-trade-journal.md)

- Filling in the whole row after the trade. The "Before" fields only work if they are written first.
- Logging only the trades that feel worth logging. Missing trades make the statistics look better than the truth. Log every trade, including the small ones and the rule breaks.
- Leaving out fees. On small accounts and short trades, fees are a visible part of 1R.

### [Reviewing your trades](../04-psychology-journal-and-paper-trading/05-reviewing-your-trades.md)

- Judging a decision by its result. Ask "did I follow the plan?" first, and look at the R second.
- Changing the plan after every loss. One change a week, based on a pattern in several trades.
- Reading a by-setup table of 10 trades as proof. Treat it as a question to keep testing.

### [Setting up paper trading](../04-psychology-journal-and-paper-trading/06-paper-trading-setup.md)

- Using the simulator's huge balance for sizing. A 1% risk of US$1,000,000 teaches you nothing about 1% of your own account.
- Skipping the journal because the money is fake. The journal is the point of the exercise.
- Counting every touch as a fill. The more fills the simulator gives you, the more your paper results will beat live ones.


## Part 5: Reading charts

### [Candles, bars and timeframes](../05-reading-charts/01-candles-bars-and-timeframes.md)

- Treating a candle's colour as a prediction. Colour only says that the close was above or below the open.
- Reading a gap as part of the body. A candle opens at the open price, which can be far from the previous close ([Gaps](../05-reading-charts/10-gaps.md)).
- Comparing candles across timeframes. A long daily body and a long weekly body are different sizes of move.

### [Trend and market structure](../05-reading-charts/02-trend-and-market-structure.md)

- Marking a swing point too soon. A swing high is not a swing high until the bars after it have confirmed it.
- Calling a trend from two points. Two swing highs and two swing lows are the minimum, and three each is better.
- Moving the invalidation level after the price breaks it. Write the level down before the trade.

### [Support and resistance](../05-reading-charts/03-support-and-resistance.md)

- Drawing a thin line and treating a wick through it as a break. Use a zone and a close.
- Drawing levels from the whole chart. Old levels from many months ago have weaker links to today's traders than levels from the last few months.
- Placing the stop exactly at the level. A stop right on the zone is where many other stops sit, and noise can trigger it. Put it past the zone, as 106.90 sits below 107.16.

### [Volume and relative volume](../05-reading-charts/04-volume-and-relative-volume.md)

- Reading volume without the average. 3 million shares means nothing until you know the stock's usual volume.
- Using the whole-day average to judge a morning bar. Volume is highest at the open, so compare the same time of day.
- Treating one heavy bar as a signal by itself. Heavy volume after a long fall can be a selling climax or the start of a bigger fall, and the volume alone does not say which.

### [Moving averages](../05-reading-charts/05-moving-averages.md)

- Forgetting the lag. An average always signals late, so decide how much of a move you accept giving back before you choose a length.
- Trading crossovers in a range. The signal flips back and forth, and each flip costs spread and slippage ([Bid, ask and the order book](../01-how-markets-work/04-bid-ask-and-the-order-book.md)).
- Picking the length that fit the past best. That is the data-snooping problem above.

### [Momentum indicators: RSI, MACD and stochastics](../05-reading-charts/06-momentum-indicators.md)

- Selling because RSI is above 70. In a strong trend it can stay there.
- Using the default settings without testing the length. A shorter length reacts faster and signals more often, with more false signals.
- Trading every MACD cross. In a range the crosses come in pairs that lose money.

### [Volatility indicators: ATR, Bollinger and Keltner](../05-reading-charts/07-volatility-indicators.md)

- Setting a stop inside the normal noise. If ATR is 0.40 and the stop is 0.30 from the entry, ordinary movement can hit it.
- Reading a band touch as a reversal. In a strong trend the price walks along the upper band.
- Mixing up the two standard deviations. The bands here divide by 20; pandas divides by 19 unless you set `ddof=0`.

### [VWAP and anchored VWAP](../05-reading-charts/08-vwap-and-anchored-vwap.md)

- Using VWAP on a daily chart. A daily bar has one price, so the line means nothing. Use the intraday VWAP, or an anchored one.
- Treating the VWAP as a prediction. It is the average of what has already traded.
- Forgetting the early bars. In the first minutes the VWAP is the first few prints.

### [Chart patterns and the evidence](../05-reading-charts/09-chart-patterns-and-the-evidence.md)

- Seeing a pattern before it is complete. A head and shoulders is only a head and shoulders once the right shoulder has formed.
- Skipping the stop because the pattern "must" work. Every pattern here has a failure on the same chart.
- Taking the measured move as a promise. It is a way to compare reward with risk, not a forecast.

### [Gaps](../05-reading-charts/10-gaps.md)

- Trading every gap fill as a rule. Some gaps are never filled, as on February 24.
- Ignoring the gap when you size a position. The stop is not where you will get out.
- Calling a type at the open. Wait for the volume and the next bars.

### [Multiple timeframes and relative strength](../05-reading-charts/11-multi-timeframe-and-relative-strength.md)

- Using too many timeframes. Weekly, daily and one shorter one are enough.
- Letting the shorter chart overrule the weekly stop. Decide the invalidation level on the chart you trade, and respect the larger one.
- Reading relative strength as direction. A stock can lag an index while rising.


## Part 6: ICT and smart money concepts, tested

### [What ICT and smart money concepts claim](../06-ict-and-smart-money-tested/01-what-ict-and-smc-claim.md)

- Treating a teacher's definition as the only one. Block, gap and structure rules vary by teacher, so write your own rule down before you test it.
- Marking structure on a chart you already know the end of. Cover the right side of the chart and mark bar by bar.
- Counting a wick through a swing point as a break when your own rule says "close".

### [Liquidity and stop runs](../06-ict-and-smart-money-tested/02-liquidity-and-stop-runs.md)

- Calling every wick through a high a sweep. Without the close-back rule, a breakout that continues is also a "sweep", and the idea cannot be wrong.
- Entering at the sweep bar's close, before the structure shift confirms it.

### [Fair value gaps](../06-ict-and-smart-money-tested/03-fair-value-gaps.md)

- Marking every three-bar gap. Without a displacement rule, small gaps appear all day and the method can explain any move.
- Putting the limit at the CE and assuming it fills. In the example the low stopped 0.20 above the CE, so the order would have missed.
- Treating "gap filled" as proof of the idea. Compare with all bars first.

### [Order blocks and breakers](../06-ict-and-smart-money-tested/04-order-blocks-and-breakers.md)

- Picking the block after seeing the move. The last down candle is only identifiable once the displacement has happened, so you can only trade the retest, and you must have the rule written before.
- Drawing the zone wider after price trades through it. A zone that stretches to include the wick fits any outcome.

### [Sessions, kill zones and premium and discount](../06-ict-and-smart-money-tested/05-sessions-kill-zones-and-premium-discount.md)

- Forgetting the daylight saving gap. For three weeks in March and one in October, London is 4 hours ahead of Toronto, so a rule based on London time moves on your clock.
- Applying a 7 to 10 a.m. window to stocks without noticing that most of it is thin pre-market trading.
- Choosing the swing after seeing the pullback. The swing low and high must be fixed by a rule (lesson 1) before you draw the levels.

### [Testing smart money claims](../06-ict-and-smart-money-tested/06-testing-smc-claims.md)

- Judging a method on its best trades. Count every instance of the rule, including those the chart no longer shows.
- Changing the rule after a bad result and testing again on the same data. That is a new variant and it counts toward step 5.
- Reading a high hit rate as an edge without the payoff and the baseline.


## Part 7: Swing trading

### [What swing trading is](../07-swing-trading/01-what-swing-trading-is.md)

- Holding a day trade overnight because it went wrong. The stop was the plan, and a hold you did not plan has no stop.
- Counting every dip as a setup. A setup has written rules. If you cannot say which of the three it is, skip it.
- Ignoring the calendar. A trade that meets an earnings report has a different risk from one that does not.

### [Scanners and watchlists](../07-swing-trading/02-scanners-and-watchlists.md)

- Treating the scanner output as a buy list. It is a list of candidates, and the chart review decides.
- A watchlist of 100 names. You will not look at them properly. Keep 10 to 30 and drop a name when it breaks its setup.
- Using a different set of filters every day. You cannot judge a setup's record if its filter keeps changing.

### [Market regime filters](../07-swing-trading/03-market-regime-filters.md)

- Using the filter on each stock and not on the market. A stock above its own 200-day average in a falling market still gets pulled down.
- Changing the filter after a bad month. Pick the number, write it down, and review it once a quarter.
- Reading a filter as a forecast. It says which rule set applies. The market can fall the day after a favourable reading.

### [Trend pullback setups](../07-swing-trading/04-trend-pullback-setups.md)

- Buying the dip before the trigger bar.
- Skipping the room test because the chart looks good. Without it you take trades that pay about 1R and lose 1R.
- Moving the stop down. A wider stop is a bigger loss than the size allows.

### [Breakouts and bases](../07-swing-trading/05-breakouts-and-bases.md)

- Buying an intraday poke above the pivot. Many are above it at 11 a.m. and below it at 4 p.m. Wait for the close.
- Counting a flat patch as a base. A 6-bar pause is not one.
- Chasing a stock far past the pivot. The rule skips an open more than 2 ATR above it.

### [Short-term mean reversion](../07-swing-trading/06-short-term-mean-reversion.md)

- Buying every drop. The filter and the first-close rule keep out a stock that stays weak.
- Averaging down. A second buy doubles the risk on a failing trade.
- Skipping the stop. The size was set from it.

### [Entries, stops and targets](../07-swing-trading/07-entries-stops-and-targets.md)

- Choosing the entry order after seeing the price. Decide it in the plan.
- Setting a target from what you hope for. Take it from a level on the chart, or from a fixed R multiple, and write it down.
- Placing the stop after the fill and being away from the screen. Use a bracket order, or place the stop in the same minute.

### [Managing the trade](../07-swing-trading/08-managing-the-trade.md)

- Widening the stop because "it will come back". The size was set from the original stop.
- Moving to break-even too early. If you use it, set the trigger at 1.5R or more and measure the cost.
- Holding through earnings without deciding. Look at the calendar when you scan.


## Part 8: Day trading

### [How day trading differs](../08-day-trading/01-how-day-trading-differs.md)

- Using the swing stop size on an intraday chart. A 4% stop is too wide for a trade you plan to close in two hours, and the position it allows is too small to matter.
- Sizing from risk and forgetting buying power. The order may be rejected, or you may end the day with less risk than planned.
- Trading the midday hours because the screen is open. Fewer setups appear then, and each costs the same fees.

### [Premarket prep](../08-day-trading/02-premarket-prep.md)

- Chasing a gap with no catalyst. A big move with no news has no stated reason to continue.
- Writing levels after the open. By then the price is moving and you pick the levels that fit the bar you want.
- Building a list of 20 names. You can watch two to five at the open, so cut the list to that.

### [Intraday charts and VWAP](../08-day-trading/03-intraday-charts-and-vwap.md)

- Reading VWAP in the first minutes. With little volume, the line is the first few bars, and a cross means little.
- Using a different VWAP from the one you tested. A line that includes premarket trades is not the same line.
- Trading the 1-minute chart with a 5-minute stop. Decide the bar size first, then place the stop in that chart's terms.

### [The opening range breakout](../08-day-trading/04-opening-range-breakout.md)

- Taking both directions. The first bar's colour picks the side, and a short on a green first bar is a different trade.
- Moving the stop to "give it room" after the entry. The size was computed from the stop, so a wider stop breaks the 1R.
- Entering on the first touch of a level in the first minute with no rule for the bar. Use the 5-minute bar and the buy stop.

### [VWAP reclaims and fades](../08-day-trading/05-vwap-reclaims-and-fades.md)

- Buying the first cross above VWAP in the first 30 minutes. VWAP is close to the price then, so a cross says little (lesson 3).
- Fading a stock with a fresh catalyst and rising volume. The news can keep pushing it, and the line follows slowly.
- Setting the target at VWAP when VWAP is only a few cents away. The reward is smaller than the costs.

### [News, momentum and halts](../08-day-trading/06-news-momentum-and-halts.md)

- Buying the first spike with a market order. The spread is widest and the price is least stable in the first minute.
- Sizing as if the stop would fill. A halt can move the open price through the stop.
- Shorting a stock that has already fallen 10% and expecting to hit the bid. Rule 201 removes that option.

### [Level 2 and time and sales](../08-day-trading/07-level-2-and-time-and-sales.md)

- Treating a large displayed order as a wall that will hold. It can be cancelled or can be one part of a larger hidden order.
- Reading the tape without size. Fifty 100-share prints at the ask can be less than one 5,000-share print at the bid.
- Paying for depth data you do not use. Decide first what decision it changes.

### [Execution costs and scalping](../08-day-trading/08-execution-costs-and-scalping.md)

- Using the quoted spread as the whole cost. Add slippage on the stop exit, which is a market order.
- Judging a scalper's win rate without costs. A 55% win rate can be a loss at 60% required.
- Paying a 1.5% currency fee on every trade from a CAD account.


## Part 9: Company financials

### [Filings and EDGAR](../09-company-financials/01-filings-and-edgar.md)

- Starting with a news story about a result. Open the filing: the story is one writer's reading of it.
- Reading only the Business section. It is written to describe the company well. The Risk Factors and MD&A show what management worries about.
- Treating a 10-Q number as final. A 10-Q is unaudited, and a later 10-K can differ.

### [The income statement](../09-company-financials/02-the-income-statement.md)

- Reading net income as cash. It is an accrual figure: the order shipped in December is revenue even if the cash arrives in January. Lesson 4 shows the cash.
- Comparing EBITDA across companies without checking what each adds back. EBITDA is not a GAAP figure, and lesson 10 returns to this.
- Forgetting that a quarter's number is not a year's. Divide or compare like with like: Q4 against Q4.

### [The balance sheet](../09-company-financials/03-the-balance-sheet.md)

- Treating equity as the market value of the company. Book equity is historical cost less depreciation and write-offs, and the market cap can be several times larger or smaller.
- Calling a company safe because working capital is positive. Check what is in it: if most of the current assets are slow inventory, the cushion is weaker.
- Ignoring goodwill. A balance sheet full of goodwill from acquisitions can be written down in one quarter.

### [The cash flow statement](../09-company-financials/04-the-cash-flow-statement.md)

- Treating CFO as hard to move. Working capital shifts it: stretching payables for a quarter raises CFO without improving the business.
- Calling a rising receivables line good news. It uses cash. If receivables grow faster than revenue (Northwind's grew 20% against revenue growth of 15%), ask why.
- Reading negative CFI as bad. A growing company has negative investing cash flow because it invests. The question is what the investment earns (lesson 7).

### [How the three statements connect](../09-company-financials/05-how-the-statements-connect.md)

- Assuming every number appears once. Net income appears in two statements, and a mismatch between them means a missed line, not a rounding error.
- Forgetting the balance sheet check when you change an assumption. If assets do not equal liabilities plus equity after your change, one side was missed.
- Mixing periods. The cash flow statement covers a year. The balance sheet is a date. Use the opening and closing balance sheets to explain the changes.

### [Margins and growth](../09-company-financials/06-margins-and-growth.md)

- Subtracting two margins and calling the result a percent. From 40.0% to 42.0% is 2.0 percentage points. It is a 5% increase in the margin, which is a different statement.
- Comparing margins across industries. A 10% net margin is thin for software and good for a distributor.
- Reading one year of growth as a trend. Use a CAGR over several years, and look at what happened in the year in between.

### [Returns on capital](../09-company-financials/07-returns-on-capital.md)

- Using only year-end equity or assets. If the balance sheet changed a lot during the year, the average is fairer. Say which one you used.
- Reading a high ROE as safe. A company with little equity left after losses or buybacks can show a huge ROE.
- Comparing ROIC from two data providers without checking the formula. Use the same one for every company.

### [Debt, liquidity and solvency](../09-company-financials/08-debt-liquidity-and-solvency.md)

- Using the current ratio alone. A high ratio built on slow inventory and old receivables is weaker than it looks, which is why the quick ratio exists.
- Looking at the debt total without the due dates. US$300 million due in five years is easier than US$300 million due next quarter.
- Ignoring cash. Gross debt of US$275 million sounds heavy, but net debt of US$145 million is the figure that shows what the company really owes.

### [Shares, EPS, dilution and buybacks](../09-company-financials/09-shares-eps-dilution-and-buybacks.md)

- Using the share count from last quarter's quote page. Use the weighted average for EPS, and the latest cover page count for market cap.
- Treating diluted EPS as a forecast. It is a calculation of what EPS would be if the promises were used today.
- Calling a buyback good because EPS rose. Check what it cost, and whether the share count really fell after pay.

### [GAAP, non-GAAP and earnings quality](../09-company-financials/10-gaap-non-gaap-and-earnings-quality.md)

- Reading adjusted EPS without the reconciliation. The reconciliation tells you which costs were removed.
- Using one year of accruals as a signal. Use a trend over three or more years.
- Calling a company dishonest because a ratio looks off. A flag is a question for the notes and the MD&A.

### [Earnings season and earnings calls](../09-company-financials/11-earnings-season-and-calls.md)

- Judging a report by the headline beat or miss. Read the guidance, the margins and the cash flow.
- Comparing with the wrong consensus. Use the one published right before the report, and know whether it is for GAAP or adjusted EPS.


## Part 10: Valuation and research

### [Price multiples](../10-valuation-and-research/01-multiples.md)

- Comparing a trailing P/E with a forward P/E. State which one a data site shows before you compare.
- Using the PEG with a growth rate that includes a one-year jump. Use a multi-year estimate.
- Using P/B for a software company. Most of its value is not on the balance sheet.

### [Enterprise value and EV/EBITDA](../10-valuation-and-research/02-enterprise-value-and-ev-ebitda.md)

- Using the basic share count for equity value. Options and RSUs are claims on the company, so count them.
- Subtracting cash that is not free, such as cash held in a foreign subsidiary that would be taxed to bring home, or cash a bank must hold. Check the notes.
- Comparing EV/EBITDA across industries. Capital needs and growth differ too much.

### [Free cash flow](../10-valuation-and-research/03-free-cash-flow.md)

- Using EBITDA as free cash flow. It skips capex, tax and working capital.
- Adding interest-related items to UFCF. Interest belongs in the discount rate (lesson 4), not in the cash flow.
- Treating a one-year drop in capex as lasting. A company cannot underinvest forever.

### [Discount rates, CAPM and WACC](../10-valuation-and-research/04-discount-rates-capm-and-wacc.md)

- Using the coupon on old debt as the cost of debt. Use today's yield for that borrower.
- Using book value of equity in the WACC weights. Use market value.
- Quoting a WACC to two decimals as if it were exact. It is a range, and lesson 5 shows the range in value.

### [A DCF step by step](../10-valuation-and-research/05-dcf-step-by-step.md)

- Setting the terminal growth rate near or above the discount rate. The formula explodes, and no company outgrows the economy forever.
- Taking the exit multiple from today's market without a haircut, which copies the market's price into the answer.
- Forgetting to subtract debt (and add cash) between enterprise value and equity value.

### [Reverse DCF and market expectations](../10-valuation-and-research/06-reverse-dcf-and-expectations.md)

- Solving for one assumption while the others are unrealistic. The answer inherits their errors.
- Treating the implied number as a forecast. It is a claim to test.
- Stopping at the number. Compare it with history and peers, or it teaches nothing.

### [Comparables and industry KPIs](../10-valuation-and-research/07-comparables-and-industry-kpis.md)

- Choosing peers by industry label alone. A fast grower and a slow one in the same sector deserve different multiples.
- Mixing trailing multiples for some peers and forward ones for others.
- Using the average when one peer is far out. Use the median and look at the range.

### [Moats and qualitative research](../10-valuation-and-research/08-moats-and-qualitative-research.md)

- Naming a moat without testing it. If ROIC has not exceeded the WACC through a downturn, call it a hope.
- Reading only the business section of the 10-K. The risk factors and the proxy hold the facts that change the thesis.
- Treating an analyst target as a promise.

### [Writing a stock pitch](../10-valuation-and-research/09-writing-a-stock-pitch.md)

- Writing the thesis as a list of good facts about the company. A good company is not a good stock unless the price is right.
- Having no variant view, so that the pitch repeats the consensus.
- Leaving out what would change your mind, which makes the thesis impossible to disprove.


## Part 11: Macro and economic data

### [How the economy is measured](../11-macro-and-economic-data/01-how-the-economy-is-measured.md)

- Reading a level as news. A jobs total of 160 million is a level. The number that matters on release day is the monthly change and how it compares with the consensus.
- Mixing up percent and percentage points. A rate rising from 4.2% to 4.3% is up 0.1 percentage points, which is a 2.4% rise in the rate itself. Say which you mean.
- Treating the first print as final. Payrolls and GDP are both revised, and the revision can be larger than the surprise.

### [The Fed and the FOMC](../11-macro-and-economic-data/02-the-fed-and-the-fomc.md)

- Treating the dot plot as a promise.
- Reading the rate decision alone. When the market expected the move, the wording and the press conference carry the news.
- Using the futures odds as a forecast. They are market prices, so they include risk premiums and move with every release.

### [Interest rates and asset prices](../11-macro-and-economic-data/03-interest-rates-and-asset-prices.md)

- Using the same rate for every cash flow. A rate rise hurts distant cash more. Discount each year on its own.
- Expecting every stock to fall when rates rise. A company with cash due soon, or with profits that rise with the economy, can hold up.
- Mixing nominal and real rates. A 4% rate with 3% inflation is a real rate near 1%. Compare like with like.

### [Inflation reports: CPI, PCE and PPI](../11-macro-and-economic-data/04-inflation-reports.md)

- Treating y/y as news. A y/y figure is made of twelve months, eleven of which the market already knows. The surprise sits in the latest month.
- Mixing seasonally adjusted and unadjusted numbers. Monthly changes are quoted seasonally adjusted. The BLS computes y/y changes from unadjusted data, as the figure does, so compare like with like.
- Assuming CPI and PCE match. They differ in coverage and weights, and the gap changes from month to month.

### [The jobs report](../11-macro-and-economic-data/05-the-jobs-report.md)

- Reading the payrolls number alone. The unemployment rate and wages can point the other way, and revisions can change the headline.
- Expecting the two surveys to agree. Different samples and definitions give different monthly changes.
- Reacting to one week of claims. Weekly claims are noisy, so compare against a four-week average.

### [Growth data: GDP, retail sales and PMIs](../11-macro-and-economic-data/06-growth-data.md)

- Reading the PMI as a growth rate. A 52 is not 2% growth. It says more managers report increases than decreases.
- Treating the first GDP print as the final one. The revisions are large relative to many surprises.
- Ignoring prices in retail sales. A 0.5% rise with 0.4% price rises is a small rise in volume.

### [The economic calendar](../11-macro-and-economic-data/07-the-economic-calendar.md)

- Trusting a calendar site over the agency. Sites copy times and can be late to update. Check the agency.
- Forgetting the time zone. Calendar sites often default to UTC or the site's own zone. Set it to Eastern.
- Having no rule until the minute of release. Decide the night before, when you are not watching the price.

### [How markets react to data](../11-macro-and-economic-data/08-how-markets-react-to-data.md)

- Expecting one fixed reaction. The sign of the stock reaction to strong data depends on the regime.
- Trading the headline before the details. A jobs headline can reverse once earnings and revisions are read.
- Forgetting the move may already be priced. Prices move on the surprise relative to consensus, so a report that matches the consensus moves little.

### [The business cycle and sectors](../11-macro-and-economic-data/09-the-business-cycle-and-sectors.md)

- Treating the textbook table as a signal. It is a loose pattern from some past cycles, and it changes with each one.
- Calling a recession from one data release. The NBER uses a wide set of data, and its calls come late.
- Expecting the averages to apply to the next cycle. Expansions have run longer and shorter than the average.

### [QE, QT and liquidity](../11-macro-and-economic-data/10-qe-qt-and-liquidity.md)

- Calling QE "printing money" and stopping there. The Fed swaps reserves for securities. The effect on yields and lending is what matters, and it is debated.
- Expecting QT to be the exact reverse of QE. The mechanics and the market's starting point are different.
- Using one weekly balance sheet number as a trading signal. Weekly H.4.1 changes are noisy and have no proven link to short-term stock moves.


## Part 12: Bonds and the yield curve

### [Bond prices and yields](../12-bonds-and-the-yield-curve/01-bond-prices-and-yields.md)

- Reading "yield" as the coupon rate. The coupon rate is fixed at issue. The yield changes every day with the price.
- Comparing bonds by current yield. It leaves out the gain or loss at maturity, so compare yield to maturity.
- Forgetting accrued interest in a trade's cost. The quote is clean, and the cash that leaves your account is dirty.

### [Treasuries and TIPS](../12-bonds-and-the-yield-curve/02-treasuries-and-tips.md)

- Treating the discount rate as the yield. Convert to the bond-equivalent yield first.
- Reading breakeven inflation as a forecast. It also moves with liquidity and risk premiums.
- Assuming a TIPS always beats a regular Treasury. It gains only if inflation exceeds the breakeven.

### [The yield curve](../12-bonds-and-the-yield-curve/03-the-yield-curve.md)

- Calling a one-day dip below zero a signal. Use monthly averages.
- Treating the spread as a timer. It gives odds, not a date.
- Mixing spreads. The 2s10s and the 3m10y invert at different times, so say which one you mean.

### [Duration and convexity](../12-bonds-and-the-yield-curve/04-duration-and-convexity.md)

- Using duration for a large move. Past about 1 or 2 percentage points, add the convexity term or reprice the bond.
- Reading duration as the maturity. Macaulay duration is in years and always below maturity for a coupon bond. Modified duration measures price sensitivity.
- Forgetting that duration changes. As yield and time move, so does duration, so a hedge needs rechecking.

### [Credit spreads and high yield](../12-bonds-and-the-yield-curve/05-credit-spreads-and-high-yield.md)

- Comparing yields of bonds with different credit quality and maturity. Compare the spread to a Treasury with a similar maturity.
- Treating the yield as the return. The yield assumes no default, so subtract an expected loss before you compare it with a safer bond.
- Trusting a rating as a price signal. Ratings follow events, and spreads often widen months before a downgrade.

### [Bond ETFs, and rates against stocks](../12-bonds-and-the-yield-curve/06-bond-etfs-and-rates-vs-stocks.md)

- Using the distribution yield as the expected return. It looks back.
- Waiting for a bond ETF to return to par like a bond. It has no maturity, so there is no date when its price must come back.
- Reading an earnings yield above the bond yield as proof that stocks are cheap. The two are not the same kind of number.


## Part 13: ETFs, portfolios and factors

### [How ETFs work](../13-etfs-portfolios-and-factors/01-how-etfs-work.md)

- Treating NAV as a price you can trade at. You trade at the market price, and NAV is a value calculated from the holdings.
- Using a market order on a thinly traded ETF. The spread can be wide and the fill can be far from NAV.
- Assuming "ETF" means diversified or safe. An ETF holds whatever its rules say, including one narrow theme.

### [Index funds, fees and tracking](../13-etfs-portfolios-and-factors/02-index-funds-fees-and-tracking.md)

- Comparing the MER alone. The spread, the tracking and the currency cost (lesson 3) can matter as much.
- Judging tracking from one year. A fund can be ahead or behind its index by luck of timing, so compare several years.
- Reading a low tracking error as a low cost. Tracking error says the gap is steady, and the tracking difference says how big it is.

### [US-listed or Canadian-listed ETFs](../13-etfs-portfolios-and-factors/03-us-vs-canadian-listed-etfs.md)

- Buying a US-listed ETF in a TFSA to "avoid withholding". The TFSA gets no treaty exemption, so the 15% is lost.
- Counting the RRSP saving without the conversion cost. Add both before you choose.
- Assuming a Canadian-listed ETF has no currency risk. It holds US$ stocks unless it is hedged.

### [Leveraged and inverse ETFs](../13-etfs-portfolios-and-factors/04-leveraged-and-inverse-etfs.md)

- Reading "2x" as "twice the return over my holding period". It is twice the daily return.
- Using an inverse fund as a long-term short. Both the index and the inverse fund can lose in a choppy market.
- Looking only at the multiple and ignoring the fee and financing cost, which are higher than for index funds.

### [Asset allocation](../13-etfs-portfolios-and-factors/05-asset-allocation.md)

- Choosing a mix from past returns alone. Pick it from the loss you can bear, then check the return.
- Counting each account separately. Your mix is the sum of all accounts, including a workplace plan.
- Treating the expected returns as promises. The inputs are assumptions, and a different pair changes every row.

### [Diversification and correlation](../13-etfs-portfolios-and-factors/06-diversification-and-correlation.md)

- Counting holdings instead of exposures. Ten funds that all hold the same large US stocks are one bet.
- Using a calm-period correlation for a stress test. Use a higher one, such as 0.8 to 0.9 between stock funds.
- Expecting diversification to remove market risk. It removes company-specific risk only.

### [Rebalancing](../13-etfs-portfolios-and-factors/07-rebalancing.md)

- Rebalancing too often in a taxable account. Each sale can create a tax bill and costs, and the evidence does not show a gain from frequent trades.
- Skipping rebalancing because the winner "has momentum". Decide the rule before the move, then follow it.
- Treating the target as sacred after your goals change. Change the target on purpose, not by drift.

### [Modern portfolio theory](../13-etfs-portfolios-and-factors/08-modern-portfolio-theory.md)

- Trusting the optimizer's weights. Small changes in the expected returns move them a lot.
- Treating the Sharpe ratio as a full measure of risk for assets with rare large losses. Standard deviation understates that risk.
- Forgetting the risk-free rate in the Sharpe ratio. Return divided by risk without it rewards an asset for earning the safe rate. Cash with a 2% return and almost no risk would score very high.

### [Factor investing](../13-etfs-portfolios-and-factors/09-factor-investing.md)

- Reading a high backtested factor return as a forecast. The research above shows returns are lower after publication.
- Tilting heavily on one factor after a good run. Factors have long cold stretches.
- Comparing a factor fund with the market over one or two years. The difference needs far longer to tell from luck.

### [A long-term portfolio](../13-etfs-portfolios-and-factors/10-a-long-term-portfolio.md)

- Planning the location before the allocation. Fix the mix first.
- Splitting each account into the same mix. The portfolio's mix is what matters, so place funds where they are taxed least.


## Part 14: Futures

### [What futures are](../14-futures/01-what-futures-are.md)

- Thinking the contract price is what you pay. A futures price is the agreed price for later. You post a deposit, not the full value.
- Forgetting the multiplier. A 10-point move is US$50 on one micro contract in this part, not US$10.
- Holding a physically settled contract into delivery by accident. Know the last day your broker lets you hold it.

### [Margin, leverage and mark to market](../14-futures/02-margin-leverage-and-mark-to-market.md)

- Treating margin as the amount at risk. The loss is set by the price move, and it can be larger than the margin.
- Sizing by how many contracts the margin allows. Size by the stop distance.
- Leaving only the initial margin in the account. A small adverse move then triggers a call.

### [Index futures: ES, MES, NQ and MNQ](../14-futures/03-index-futures.md)

- Using the tick value of ES for MES. Tick values differ by a factor of ten.
- Counting points, not ticks, in the formula. 8 points is 32 ticks.
- Placing the stop by the number of contracts you want. Set the stop from the chart first, then compute contracts.

### [Expiry and rolling](../14-futures/04-expiry-and-rolling.md)

- Holding to the last day. The expiring contract is thin and your broker may close it for you.
- Copying levels across the roll without the spread. A stop or target needs the gap added or removed.
- Reading a gap on a continuous chart as a trade signal. It is the stitch.

### [Contango, backwardation and basis](../14-futures/05-contango-backwardation-and-basis.md)

- Reading contango as a forecast that prices will rise. It is the cost of carry, not an expectation.
- Ignoring the basis when comparing a future with the index. A 40.50-point difference is not an arbitrage.
- Mixing the sign convention for basis. Write which one you use.

### [Futures hours and sessions](../14-futures/06-futures-hours-and-sessions.md)

- Reading the 5 p.m. to 6 p.m. ET break as part of the next day. The market is closed, and a stop cannot fill.
- Using a stock-hours chart to place a stop that must hold overnight. The overnight range is not on the chart.
- Mixing "all hours" and "regular hours" charts in one plan.

### [Futures in Canada](../14-futures/07-futures-in-canada.md)

- Assuming your account type can hold futures. Confirm it in writing from the broker.
- Quoting a fee per contract when the broker charges per side. Double it in the cost in R until you know.
- Using a US multiplier for a Canadian contract. SXF is C$200, not US$.


## Part 15: Options

### [Calls and puts](../15-options/01-calls-and-puts.md)

- Reading the quote as the total cost. A quote of 3.25 is US$325 for one contract.
- Calling an option "cheap" because the premium is small. A US$0.10 option on a US$50 stock is cheap only if the stock is likely to move far enough, and it usually is not.
- Thinking you need to exercise to profit. Selling the contract back is usually how a trader collects.

### [Payoff diagrams](../15-options/02-payoff-diagrams.md)

- Putting the breakeven at the strike. The premium moves it: a call's breakeven is above the strike, a put's below.
- Forgetting the 100. The diagram's units are US$ per share. Multiply by 100 for a contract.
- Treating a short option's maximum gain as the goal. The seller's best case is small and capped, and the worst case is large. Part 3's R and expectancy lessons apply here as well ([Win rate, payoff and expectancy](../03-risk-and-position-sizing/03-win-rate-payoff-and-expectancy.md)).

### [The options chain and moneyness](../15-options/03-the-chain-and-moneyness.md)

- Choosing by the last price. A contract that traded once at 1.30 yesterday can have a bid of 1.10 today. Use the bid and ask.
- Trading a contract with a wide spread and no open interest. The cost of getting out can be larger than the premium.
- Mixing up ITM for calls and puts. A strike below the stock is ITM for a call and OTM for a put.

### [What sets an option's price](../15-options/04-what-sets-an-option-price.md)

- Treating the model price as the right price. It is the price for a given volatility. A different volatility gives a different price.
- Reading the risk-neutral probability \(p\) or \(N(d_2)\) as the real chance the stock rises. They come from the pricing argument.
- Using days in place of years for \(T\). Divide the days by 365.

### [Put-call parity](../15-options/05-put-call-parity.md)

- Using parity with different strikes or expiries. It needs the same strike and the same expiry.
- Forgetting the dividend. A stock that pays one before expiry moves the call and put apart.
- Treating a gap as free money. After spreads, commissions and borrow costs, most gaps are gone.

### [Delta and gamma](../15-options/06-delta-and-gamma.md)

- Reading delta as an exact probability. It is a rough proxy and differs from the model's own \(N(d_2)\).
- Forgetting that delta changes. A hedge that is neutral at US$50 is not neutral at US$53.
- Ignoring the 100. A delta of 0.56 is 56 shares for one contract.

### [Theta, vega and rho](../15-options/07-theta-vega-and-rho.md)

- Thinking time decay is steady. It speeds up near expiry, which is why a short-dated option can lose most of its value in the last week.
- Buying an option into an event because the move looks likely. The price already contains a high volatility, and it falls once the event passes.
- Quoting a Greek per share when you hold contracts. Multiply by 100 and by the number of contracts.

### [Implied volatility](../15-options/08-implied-volatility.md)

- Reading IV as a forecast. It is the market's price for movement, and the stock can move more or less.
- Comparing IV across expiries without care. A one-week and a three-month IV answer different questions.
- Quoting a daily move with the yearly IV. Divide by \(\sqrt{252}\) for one day.

### [Covered calls and cash-secured puts](../15-options/09-covered-calls-and-cash-secured-puts.md)

- Selling a call on shares you do not want to lose. A rally takes the shares at 52.50.
- Judging a cash-secured put by the premium. The risk is the stock falling, not the US$180.
- Counting premium as profit before the position is closed or expires.

### [Vertical spreads](../15-options/10-vertical-spreads.md)

- Calling a credit spread "safe" because it usually wins. The worst case is larger than the best case.
- Sizing by the credit. The risk is the width less the credit.
- Choosing strikes by price alone. A wide spread near the money is a different bet from a narrow one far away.

### [Straddles and strangles](../15-options/11-straddles-and-strangles.md)

- Buying a straddle right before an event without comparing its price with the expected move.
- Ignoring IV crush. A correct call on the size of the move can still lose.
- Sizing by the premium of one leg. The risk is the debit of both.

### [Iron condors and butterflies](../15-options/12-iron-condors-and-butterflies.md)

- Selling a condor for the credit and placing no exit. Decide the loss level before you enter.
- Choosing a very narrow butterfly and expecting to hit it. The zone is about 3.80 wide at the money.
- Holding a condor into the last day with a short strike near the stock.

### [Calendars and diagonals](../15-options/13-calendars-and-diagonals.md)

- Treating the model table as a quote. The far leg's real value depends on the IV that day.
- Holding the short leg through ex-dividend while it is in the money.
- Opening a calendar before an event inside the near leg's life without allowing for the jump.

### [Assignment, exercise and 0DTE](../15-options/14-assignment-exercise-and-0dte.md)

- Keeping a deep in-the-money short call through an ex-dividend date. Assignment is likely.
- Sizing a same-day option below its true risk. The premium can go to zero in an hour, so 1R is the premium plus fees.


## Part 17: Commodities, forex and REITs

### [Commodity markets](../17-commodities-forex-and-reits/01-commodity-markets.md)

- Reading a futures price as the price of the physical good today. The front contract and the spot price can differ, and later contracts differ again.
- Treating a hedge as a way to make money. It fixes a price. A producer that hedges at US$70 gives up the gain if oil goes to US$80.
- Mixing barrels and contracts. One WTI contract is 1,000 barrels, so a US$1 move is US$1,000 per contract, not US$1.

### [What moves oil and gold](../17-commodities-forex-and-reits/02-what-moves-oil-and-gold.md)

- Trading the headline instead of the surprise. An inventory build that was forecast moves little.
- Treating one driver as the answer. Oil can fall on a build and rise on a weaker dollar in the same hour.
- Assuming gold must fall when real yields rise. It is a pressure that other forces can overpower.

### [Commodity ETFs and roll costs](../17-commodities-forex-and-reits/03-commodity-etfs-and-roll-costs.md)
