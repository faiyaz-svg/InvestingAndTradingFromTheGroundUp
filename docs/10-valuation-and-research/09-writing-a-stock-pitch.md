# Writing a stock pitch

<span class="tag tag-skill">Skill</span> <span class="tag tag-review">Review</span> <span class="tag tag-part">VAL</span>

After this lesson you can write a one-page stock pitch with a thesis, a variant view, catalysts, a valuation range, risks and the facts that would change your mind.

Before this: [Moats and qualitative research](08-moats-and-qualitative-research.md), [Reverse DCF and market expectations](06-reverse-dcf-and-expectations.md). You need: a calculator.

## The idea

A **stock pitch** is a short written case for owning (or not owning) a stock at today's price. It forces you to say what you believe, why the market is wrong or right, what the stock is worth, and what would prove you wrong. The pitch also becomes a record: when you review it a year later, you can see which parts of your reasoning held.

The pitch below is for the made-up company Northwind from part 9. It is a worked example of the form, not a recommendation about any real security. A pitch can also be a case for staying away at the current price, which is the honest answer to many stocks.

## How it works

A pitch has six parts. Each is one short paragraph or a few lines.

1. Thesis: what the company is and why it will do better or worse than expected, in two or three sentences.
2. Variant view: where you differ from what the price implies. This is the most important part. If your view equals the market's, you have no edge. The reverse DCF (lesson 6) gives the market's view in numbers.
3. Catalysts: dated events that could make the market notice, such as earnings reports, a product launch or a margin target. A good idea with no catalyst can stay wrong for years.
4. Valuation: a range of values from scenarios, compared with the price (lesson 5 and lesson 7).
5. Risks: what could go wrong and how much it would cost, ranked by size.
6. What would change your mind: specific numbers that would make you drop the thesis, written before you own the stock.

### Worked example: a pitch for a made-up company

Price US$50.00 (the close before the FY2025 report). Diluted shares 101.5 million. WACC 9.1%.

Thesis. Northwind sells tools with a 23.2% ROIC against a 9.1% WACC, and it grew revenue 15% in FY2025 with a rising margin. It is a good business.

Variant view. The price needs revenue growth of 19.4% a year for five years at the base margins (lesson 6). We expect about 11% a year, falling to 7%. We think the market is paying for growth the company is unlikely to keep.

Catalysts. Each quarterly report in FY2026 tests growth and the margin path (20.4% in FY2025 to a base case of 24% by FY2030). A slowdown in capex from 6.5% to 4.2% of revenue lifts cash flow.

Valuation. Three scenarios, each valued with the DCF of lesson 5:

| Scenario | Revenue growth, FY2026 to FY2030 | EBIT margin in FY2030 | Value per share | Probability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bear | 10% falling to 5% | 21% | US$28.75 | 25% |
| Base | 15% falling to 7% | 24% | US$39.42 | 50% |
| Bull | 20% falling to 12% | 27% | US$53.24 | 25% |

Expected value = 0.25 x 28.75 + 0.50 x 39.42 + 0.25 x 53.24 = US$40.21. The price is 24% above it. From US$50.00 the bear case is a fall of 42.5% and the bull case a gain of 6.5%.

Risks. A faster-growing competitor or a price war (margin), a recession that cuts customers' capital spending (growth), and a rise in rates (a higher WACC: at 10.1% the base value is US$33.33).

What would change our mind. Revenue growth of 17% or more for two years in a row with an EBIT margin of 23% or more would move us toward the bull case. A price near US$36.50 would give the expected value a 10% margin (40.21 / 1.10 = 36.55) and make it worth a closer look. Either one puts the stock back on the list.

Conclusion. A good company at a price that already pays for the bull case. Pass at US$50.00, and revisit near US$36.50 or on evidence of faster growth.

</div>

### What makes a pitch good

- The numbers in the valuation come from stated assumptions, so a reader can change one and see the effect.
- The risks name the ones that would hurt the thesis most, and go beyond generic ones.
- The "change my mind" line has numbers and dates, and it is written before you buy. People rarely abandon an idea they own without a rule set in advance ([Rules and checklists](../04-psychology-journal-and-paper-trading/03-rules-and-checklists.md)).
- It says what was assumed about the discount rate and the terminal value, since most of the value sits there (lesson 5).

Pitches are also an interview task. Market and stock pitch interviews<!-- link: ../22-careers-interviews-and-ai/04-market-and-stock-pitch-interviews.md --> covers how to give one aloud.

## Common mistakes

- Writing the thesis as a list of good facts about the company. A good company is not a good stock unless the price is right.
- Having no variant view, so that the pitch repeats the consensus.
- Leaving out what would change your mind, which makes the thesis impossible to disprove.

## Try it

1\. In the Northwind scenarios, change the probabilities to 40% bear, 40% base and 20% bull. What is the expected value, and how far is the price above it?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

0.40 x 28.75 + 0.40 x 39.42 + 0.20 x 53.24 = 11.50 + 15.77 + 10.65 = US$37.92. The price is 50.00 / 37.92 - 1 = 31.9% above it.

</details>

2\. Using the base probabilities (25/50/25), at what price would the expected value be 15% above the price?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

40.21 / 1.15 = US$34.97.

</details>

3\. Write the one-sentence variant view for a stock at US$40 whose reverse DCF implies 12% growth for five years while you expect 6%.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

"The price needs 12% growth for five years and I expect 6%, so the market is paying for growth the company is unlikely to deliver." Any version that states both numbers is fine.

</details>

## Part check

1\. Stock G trades at US$60 with EPS of US$2.50. Find the P/E and the earnings yield.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

P/E = 60 / 2.50 = 24.0. Earnings yield = 2.50 / 60 = 4.17%.

</details>

2\. A company has a market cap of US$3,000 million, debt of US$800 million, cash of US$300 million and preferred stock of US$100 million. EBITDA is US$300 million. Find EV and EV/EBITDA.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

EV = 3,000 + 800 + 100 - 300 = US$3,600 million. EV/EBITDA = 3,600 / 300 = 12.0.

</details>

3\. EBIT is US$120 million, the tax rate is 25%, D&A is US$20 million, capex is US$45 million and net working capital rises by US$10 million. Find unlevered FCF.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

120 x 0.75 + 20 - 45 - 10 = 90 + 20 - 45 - 10 = US$55 million.

</details>

4\. The risk-free rate is 4.5%, beta is 1.1 and the ERP is 5.0%. Equity is 80% of funding, and the after-tax cost of debt is 4.0%. Find the cost of equity and WACC.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

\(k_e\) = 4.5% + 1.1 x 5.0% = 10.0%. WACC = 0.80 x 10.0% + 0.20 x 4.0% = 8.0% + 0.8% = 8.8%.

</details>

5\. Year 5 UFCF is US$60 million, WACC is 9% and terminal growth is 3%. Find the terminal value and its present value today.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

TV = 60 x 1.03 / (0.09 - 0.03) = US$1,030 million. PV = 1,030 / 1.09^5 = 1,030 / 1.5386 = US$669.4 million.

</details>

6\. A reverse DCF says a stock's price needs 18% revenue growth for five years. The company's best year was 9%. What does that tell you?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The price assumes growth at twice the company's best year, held for five years. Unless you have a specific reason to expect that (a new market, a product), the price is asking for more than the record supports.

</details>

7\. An analyst's price target is US$40 on a stock at US$38, and your required return is 9%. Does the target alone justify owning it?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Upside = 40 / 38 - 1 = 5.3%, below 9%. No. The target also reflects the analyst's incentives and one valuation, so build your own.

</details>

## Key terms

- **Stock pitch**: a short written case for owning, or avoiding, a stock at the current price.
- **Thesis**: the main reason the stock will do better or worse than expected.
- **Variant view**: where your view differs from what the price implies.
- **Catalyst**: a dated event that could make the market change its view.
- **Scenario analysis**: valuing a company under several sets of assumptions, with a probability on each.
- **Margin of safety**: a gap between the value you estimate and the price you pay.
