# Market and stock pitch interviews

<span class="tag tag-career">Career</span> <span class="tag tag-part">CAREER</span>

After this lesson you can answer "walk me through the markets", give a 90-second stock pitch aloud with numbers, and handle the follow-up questions.

Before this: [Writing a stock pitch](../10-valuation-and-research/09-writing-a-stock-pitch.md) and [Earnings season and earnings calls](../09-company-financials/11-earnings-season-and-calls.md). You need: a calculator.

## The idea

An interviewer at a fund says, "Walk me through the markets this week," and a minute later, "Pitch me a stock." Both questions test the same thing: whether you follow markets and can reason aloud from numbers. They are not tests of memory. A pitch that is clear and honest about its risks beats a clever one you cannot defend.

## How it works

### Walk me through the markets

Use a fixed order so you do not ramble. Check each item before the interview, because the answer is about this week, not a template.

1. Stocks: how the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq-100 moved, and which sectors led.
2. Rates: the 10-year Treasury yield and the next central bank decision.
3. The dollar and Canadian dollar: whether USD strengthened and why it matters for a Canadian investor.
4. Commodities: oil and gold, in a sentence.
5. Volatility: where the VIX is relative to its recent range.
6. One event that mattered (a data release, an earnings report) and what surprised the market about it. News moves a price only against expectations (part 9, earnings calls).
7. What you are watching next week, with a date.

Finish with a view if asked, and say what would change it. Never quote a level you did not check that morning.

### Pitch me a stock

Use the six parts from part 10: thesis, variant view, catalysts, valuation, risks, what would change your mind. Spoken, it runs 200 to 250 words. The pitch below (about 170 words) uses the made-up company Northwind. It is a form to copy, not a view on any real stock.

### Worked example: a 90-second pitch for a made-up company

"Northwind makes industrial tools. It earns 23.2% on invested capital against a 9.1% cost of capital, and grew revenue 15% last year. It is a good business at US$50.00 a share. My question is the price. The price needs revenue growth of 19.4% a year for five years. I expect about 11% a year, falling to 7%. So I think the market pays for growth Northwind is unlikely to keep.

My three scenarios give US$28.75, US$39.42 and US$53.24, weighted 25%, 50% and 25%. That is an expected value of US$40.21, about 20% below the price. From US$50.00 the bear case loses 42.5% and the bull case gains 6.5%. I would pass at US$50.00 and look again near US$36.50, a 10% margin below the expected value. Each quarterly report tests growth and margin.

The main risks: a price war cutting margin, and a recession cutting customers' spending. If revenue grows 17% or more for two years with a margin of 23% or more, I would move toward the bull case."

Numbers to check: 40.21 / 50.00 - 1 = -19.6%. 28.75 / 50.00 - 1 = -42.5%. 53.24 / 50.00 - 1 = +6.5%. 40.21 / 1.10 = 36.55.

</div>

Speak it once, then time it. Under 2 minutes is the target.

### Follow-ups to prepare

| Question | Answer pattern |
|---|---|
| Why is the market wrong? | State the implied number (19.4% growth) and your number (11%). |
| What if rates rise a point? | Re-run at the higher WACC. At 10.1% the base value is US$33.33, which is 15.4% below US$39.42. |
| What is the downside? | Give the bear value and the percentage from the price. |
| What would make you buy? | A price, and the evidence that would move you to the bull case. |
| What is the biggest assumption? | The terminal value is 80% of enterprise value in the base case ([A DCF step by step](../10-valuation-and-research/05-dcf-step-by-step.md)). |
| Do you own it? | Answer honestly. Say paper or real, and how big. |

Two things lose interviews. One is not knowing the number behind your claim. The other is defending a thesis against every fact, so say "that would change my view" when a fact does.

## Common mistakes

- Telling a story about the company without the price. A good company is not a good stock at any price.
- Quoting this week's levels from memory instead of checking them.
- Skipping the risks. The interviewer will find one, and it looks worse if you did not.

## Try it

1\. Using the scenario table, what is the expected value with probabilities 30% bear, 50% base and 20% bull?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

0.30 x 28.75 + 0.50 x 39.42 + 0.20 x 53.24 = 8.625 + 19.71 + 10.648 = US$38.98.

</details>

2\. The interviewer asks, "What if the WACC is 10.1%?" Give the answer from the sensitivity table.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

US$33.33 at a terminal growth of 3.0%, which is 15.4% below the base US$39.42 and 33.3% below the US$50.00 price.

</details>

3\. Write the one-sentence variant view for a made-up Stock C at US$80 whose reverse DCF needs 14% growth when you expect 8%.

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

"The price needs 14% growth and I expect 8%, so the market is paying for growth the company is unlikely to deliver." Both numbers must appear.

</details>

4\. Record the pitch aloud and time it. How many words fit in 90 seconds at 150 words a minute?

<details class="solution" markdown>
<summary>Answer</summary>

150 x 1.5 = 225 words. A pitch of 250 words runs about 100 seconds, so cut it to about 225.

</details>

## Key terms

- **Market walkthrough**: a short, ordered account of what moved in stocks, rates, currency, commodities and volatility, and why.
- **Spoken pitch**: a stock pitch of about 90 seconds with thesis, variant view, valuation, risks and a change-my-mind line.
